PCOS Education Hub

Understand PCOS, learn what to monitor, and explore evidence-based care.

First, remember this

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common hormonal condition that can affect menstrual cycles, skin, hair growth, fertility and metabolic health. Symptoms vary widely. PCOS is manageable, and having it is not your fault. A healthcare professional can help you understand your symptoms and choose appropriate care.

Understanding PCOS

The essentials everyone should know.

What is PCOS?

PCOS is a hormonal condition associated with irregular ovulation and higher androgen levels in some people. It may also be associated with insulin resistance and increased risk of certain metabolic conditions. It can affect reproductive health and overall well-being.

Despite its name, ovarian cysts are not required for a PCOS diagnosis.

Common Signs and Symptoms

  • Irregular, infrequent or absent periods.
  • Excess facial or body hair.
  • Persistent acne or oily skin.
  • Thinning hair on the scalp.
  • Difficulty ovulating or becoming pregnant.
  • Weight changes or difficulty managing weight in some people.

Not everyone experiences all these symptoms. Similar symptoms can also have other causes.

Causes and Contributing Factors

  • The exact cause of PCOS is not fully understood.
  • Family history and genetic factors may contribute.
  • Hormonal changes, including higher androgen levels, are involved.
  • Insulin resistance is common in PCOS and may contribute to hormonal changes.

PCOS can affect people at different body sizes. It is not simply caused by eating certain foods or by a lack of exercise.

How Is PCOS Diagnosed?

There is no single test that confirms PCOS in every adult. Clinicians assess symptoms and medical history and may use examinations, blood tests and, when appropriate, ultrasound.

In adults, diagnosis generally requires at least two of three features: signs of androgen excess, irregular ovulation or periods, and polycystic ovarian appearance on ultrasound, after other causes are considered. An ultrasound is not always necessary.

Everyday Health and Lifestyle

Practical habits that support general health without unrealistic restrictions.

Balanced Eating

A sustainable eating pattern may include:

  • Vegetables and a variety of whole fruits.
  • Whole grains such as oats, whole-wheat roti and millets.
  • Protein sources such as dal, beans, eggs, fish, tofu or lean meat.
  • Nuts, seeds and suitable sources of unsaturated fats.
  • Water and regular meals that fit your needs and preferences.

There is no single diet proven best for everyone with PCOS. Avoid unnecessary food bans or extreme diets. A registered dietitian can help with individual needs.

Physical Activity

Choose activities you can enjoy and maintain, such as:

  • Brisk walking or other aerobic activity.
  • Cycling, swimming or dancing.
  • Strength training suited to your ability.
  • Yoga, stretching or other movement you enjoy.

Regular movement can support metabolic health, mood and general well-being. Benefits can occur even without weight loss. Start at a comfortable level and build gradually.

Sleep and Emotional Well-being

  • Keep a consistent sleep and wake schedule where possible.
  • Make time for rest and activities that help manage stress.
  • Seek support if anxiety, low mood or body-image concerns affect daily life.
  • Tell your clinician if you have persistent sleep problems or significant daytime tiredness.

Emotional well-being is an important part of PCOS care, not an afterthought.

Cycle Tracking

  • Record the first day of each period.
  • Note how long bleeding lasts and whether it is unusually heavy.
  • Track symptoms such as pain, acne or changes in hair growth.
  • Share your records with a healthcare professional when needed.

Cycle apps estimate dates; they cannot confirm ovulation. With irregular cycles, predictions may be especially inaccurate.

Treatment and Long-term Health

Care is individual. A clinician can help decide what is appropriate for you.

Treatment Options

Treatment depends on symptoms, health history and whether pregnancy is desired.

  • Personalized lifestyle support for general and metabolic health.
  • Hormonal medicines may help manage irregular periods or androgen-related symptoms.
  • Metformin may be considered for selected metabolic indications.
  • Specific medicines and treatments can be used to support ovulation when pregnancy is desired.
  • Skin and hair symptoms may need their own treatment.

Medicines have different benefits, risks and restrictions. Do not start, stop or change medication or supplements without appropriate medical advice.

Health Checks to Discuss

Depending on your history and clinical needs, a healthcare professional may discuss:

  • Blood glucose testing for diabetes risk.
  • Blood pressure and cholesterol assessment.
  • Menstrual patterns and protection of the uterine lining when periods are very infrequent.
  • Sleep, mood and emotional well-being.
  • Fertility goals and pregnancy planning, if relevant.

PCOS is associated with increased risks of some metabolic conditions. Regular, individualized follow-up can help identify concerns early.

PCOS: Myths vs Facts

Clear up some common misunderstandings.

Myth Fact
Everyone with PCOS has ovarian cysts. No. Ovarian cysts are not required for diagnosis.
Only people with excess body weight get PCOS. PCOS can affect people at any body size.
PCOS always causes infertility. PCOS can affect ovulation, but many people with PCOS become pregnant, sometimes with treatment.
A special diet can cure PCOS. No diet has been established as a universal cure. Sustainable habits can support health and symptom management.
An irregular period automatically means PCOS. Other conditions can cause irregular periods. A healthcare professional can assess the cause.
PCOS is only about reproductive health. It can also relate to metabolic health, sleep and emotional well-being.

When Should You Seek Medical Advice?

Do not ignore these signs

  • Your periods are persistently irregular, or you go around three months without a period and pregnancy is not the explanation.
  • You develop new or rapidly worsening facial hair, scalp hair loss or severe acne.
  • You are trying to conceive and have irregular or absent periods.
  • You have persistent concerns about blood sugar, blood pressure, sleep or mood.

Seek urgent medical care for very heavy bleeding accompanied by dizziness or fainting, severe or sudden pelvic pain, or other severe symptoms. If pregnancy is possible and you have severe pain or bleeding, seek urgent assessment.

Your PCOS Care Checklist

Useful information to prepare before a medical appointment.

  • Record period dates and any changes in your cycle.
  • Write down symptoms, when they started, and how they affect you.
  • List your current medicines and supplements.
  • Ask whether glucose, cholesterol or blood-pressure checks are appropriate.
  • Discuss your priorities, including symptom relief, cycle management or pregnancy plans.
